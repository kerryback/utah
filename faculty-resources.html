Teaching with AI: materials, courses, and where they come from

A companion list to the seminar, for faculty in any discipline who want a next step. Sections run from what is free and usable this week to programs that cost money and take a cohort. Compiled 11 October 2026; AI resources move quickly, so confirm dates and fees at the source.

Kerry Back · Rice University

Free and open, usable this week

These cost nothing, require no institutional agreement, and can be opened in a browser now.

Wharton Generative AI Labs — Resources for Instructors

Ethan Mollick and Lilach Mollick, University of Pennsylvania

FreeCC BY 4.0

The closest thing to a drop-in kit. A prompt library of tested templates organized by purpose — instructor aids, student exercises, and general use — each with instructions, suggested uses, and model recommendations. Licensed CC BY 4.0, so you can adapt a prompt and hand it to students under your own name. The library carries its own warning that outputs may be wrong, which is the right posture.

The same page links a five-part crash course on practical AI for instructors and students.

gail.wharton.upenn.edu/prompt-library/

Instructors as Innovators: a future-focused approach to new AI learning opportunities, with prompts

Mollick and Mollick, April 2024

FreePaper with prompts

The single most directly usable paper on this list. It walks through four exercise types AI makes cheap — simulation, mentoring, coaching, co-creation — and for each gives a customizable prompt, guidance on running it in class, notes on assessment, and the risks. It also includes blueprints: prompts whose job is to help you write your own.

Posted on SSRN; search the title. A copy is also hosted by the University of Warwick's teaching-development fund.

MIT Sloan Teaching & Learning Technologies — AI for Teaching

FreeBusiness school specific

The only openly published set of teaching-with-AI guides written inside a business school. Five of them: getting started with AI-enhanced teaching; practical classroom strategies; four steps to design an AI-resilient learning experience; why AI detectors do not work and what to do instead; and using AI to build and analyze pre- and post-class surveys.

GMAC published a companion case on how Sloan built this — a central AI hub, a peer-to-peer faculty program, regular town halls, and a funded pilot group — which is the more useful document if you are thinking about the school rather than your own course.

gmac.com — MIT Sloan, AI in Business Education (PDF)

Harvard Business Publishing — Inspiring Minds

Free to readShort articles

A standing generative-AI section of short practitioner articles, and the lowest-barrier thing to send a skeptical colleague. Three worth starting with: how to use AI to create role-play scenarios for your students; an in-class AI exercise to help your students get hired; and AI as feedback generator. There is also a beginner's guide for educators and a set of student guidelines you can adapt.

Assignment and policy collections

Shared teaching artifacts rather than advice. All four are open; all four are weighted toward writing and the humanities, which is the gap noted at the end of this sheet.

The AI Pedagogy Project

metaLAB at Harvard, Berkman Klein Center for Internet & Society

FreeNo login

Two halves: an AI guide that introduces large language models through tutorials and key concepts, and a curated, growing collection of assignments contributed by educators. Aimed at post-secondary instructors in non-technical fields, so it is a better pointer for colleagues in management, ethics, and communication than for finance or operations.

TRAIIL — Teaching Repository for AI-Infused Learning

University of Central Florida

FreePeer reviewed

Openly licensed teaching strategies, peer reviewed, submissions accepted year-round. Entries are grouped by how much of the task the AI does: ideation and structuring, editing, task execution with human oversight, and full integration. That is also the answer to “where do I publish the thing I built,” which matters if a school wants credit for its own work.

Syllabi Policies for Generative AI

Lance Eaton, Northeastern University

FreeCrowdsourced

A collection of real syllabus language from instructors across institutions, assembled so you can see the range before writing your own. The standard answer to “what should my syllabus say.”

TextGenEd

WAC Clearinghouse, Colorado State University

FreeOpen access, peer reviewed

Thirty-four undergraduate assignments on AI literacy, rhetorical and ethical engagement, and professional writing. Composition-oriented, but the assignment write-ups are detailed enough to adapt.

Courses you can take for nothing

Self-paced, no institutional purchase, certificates optional. Useful if you want structure rather than a page of links.

Anthropic Academy

FreeSelf-paced

Four tracks are relevant. AI Fluency: Framework and Foundations is the base. AI Fluency for Educators applies it to course design, materials, and assessment. Teaching AI Fluency is built for faculty who will train other faculty, which is the one to know about if your school runs peer workshops. There is a student version you can assign. The framework behind them was developed with Rick Dakan and Joseph Feller.

The same courses are also on Coursera, free with optional certificates.

blog.coursera.org — Anthropic's five free courses

OpenAI Academy — ChatGPT for Higher Ed Faculty

FreeFree account required

A recorded series for university instructors covering AI literacy, syllabus design, and student-AI policy. Needs only a free ChatGPT account, not a subscription. Badges are awarded at 80 percent on a course assessment, and a full pathway yields a certificate.

help.openai.com — course index

AI in Education: Leveraging ChatGPT for Teaching

Wharton, on Coursera

Free to audit

The Mollick material in course form, if you prefer video and a sequence to a prompt library.

Programs with a cohort and a fee

Worth it mainly for the room: you are buying contact with faculty at other schools solving the same problem. Confirm current dates and prices at registration.

Teaching with AI

Harvard Business Publishing Education

Registration feeDegree-granting faculty only

Runs in two formats: an in-person seminar at Harvard Business School, most recently 9–10 October 2026, led by Mitchell Weiss with Dan Levy; and a virtual seminar, next listed for 14 January 2027. Content is general classroom approaches, hands-on exercises for students, student guidelines, and student perspectives. Fees vary by seminar — earlier editions were listed at $1,195 in person and $495 virtual, so treat those as indicative only. Registration is restricted to teaching faculty at degree-granting institutions.

AI Workshop for Business School Teams

AACSB

Registration feeTeams of faculty, staff, administrators

Two days, built for a mixed team from one school rather than an individual. The earlier three-part series — embedding AI into the curriculum, teaching and learning with AI, and using AI tools for your own efficiency — covered hands-on labs across several models, prompting, ethics, and the practical barriers. AACSB also ran a related curriculum workshop in Amsterdam in March 2026 and a free webinar, AI-Ready Business Schools, in August 2026.

Institute on AI, Pedagogy, and the Curriculum, 2026–27

American Association of Colleges and Universities

Application and feeInstitutional teams

Application-driven and team-based, directed by C. Edward Watson, with institute faculty including José Antonio Bowen and Bryan Alexander. This is the one a school sends a cross-disciplinary delegation to when the question is curriculum rather than a single course.

Frameworks and benchmarking

For the question “what are comparable schools actually doing.” Read these as self-reports from participating schools, not as accreditation requirements.

A Framework for Artificial Intelligence in Business Education — July 2026 update

Inspire Higher Ed, with AACSB, the Graduate Business Curriculum Roundtable, and GMAC

Free download84 schools

The benchmarking document for this sector. It now covers 84 business schools, up from 48 in January 2026 and 26 at the first edition in July 2025 — 75 percent growth in six months. Eight themes organize the exemplars, one of which is faculty development as the critical success factor.

inspirehighered.com/report gbcroundtable.org/2026aiframerworkreport

Digital Education Council resource library

FreeFrameworks and case studies

Two items are worth the visit. The AI Literacy Framework defines five dimensions with three competency levels each, which is a workable vocabulary for a program-level conversation. The Faculty Support and Resource Basket collects more than fifty practices and case studies specifically on building faculty capability in business schools — incentives, workload, microgrants, showcases.

AACSB Insights

Free

Recent pieces worth reading: Closing the AI Gap in Business Education (July 2026), Leading the AI Transition (March 2026), and How AI Is Evolving in Business Schools (February 2026). The 2025 report GenAI Adoption in Business Schools surveyed 236 deans in 56 countries and 429 faculty in 61; 47 percent reported having an AI policy, 45 percent reported none, and 29 percent of faculty did not know whether their school had one.

Books

Teaching with AI: A Practical Guide to a New Era of Human Learning

José Antonio Bowen and C. Edward Watson · Johns Hopkins University Press · 2nd edition, 2025

Purchase

The standard recommendation. Long stretches of sample prompts with responses, frameworks for building your own tools, and low-effort interventions on academic integrity. Its central move is to treat prompting as a liberal-arts skill — forming a question, judging an answer — rather than a technical one.

Co-Existence: The Next Phase of AI

Ethan Mollick · Portfolio · 20 October 2026

PurchasePublished two days before this seminar

The successor to Co-Intelligence. Its argument is that the model of a human at the center with AI as assistant is being overtaken by autonomous systems, and it asks what you do when AI is also your reader, your critic, and the gatekeeper between your work and its audience.

Handbook of Generative Artificial Intelligence in Accounting and Business Education

Springer

Purchase or library accessDiscipline specific

Edited chapters on integrating generative AI in accounting and business teaching, with the pedagogical and ethical arguments laid out at length. Closer to the disciplines in this room than most of the general literature.

Keeping up without a subscription budget

The honest answer to “how do I not fall behind” is a short reading habit rather than a course.

One Useful Thing

Ethan Mollick

Free

The one to subscribe to if you subscribe to one. Mollick tests things himself and writes up what changed, and he teaches, so the classroom implications are usually in the post.

Rhetorica

Marc Watkins, University of Mississippi

Free

The most useful skeptical voice, and he founded the Mississippi AI Institute for Teachers. His standing caution is worth keeping in view: faculty cannot be expected to carry out large-scale course redesign without funding and a community of practice alongside the guides.

Podcasts and other newsletters

Free

Teaching in Higher Ed and Tea for Teaching both ran AI episodes through 2026. Stanford publishes an AI x Education newsletter. Among newsletters Watkins recommends: Jason Gulya's The AI Edventure, Anna Mills on AI and writing pedagogy, Annette Vee, and John Warner's The Biblioracle Recommends.

What is not on this list, and why that matters

There is no open repository of business-discipline AI assignments. The collections that exist are weighted toward writing and the humanities. For finance, accounting, and operations, what is available is journal articles and paid courses, not teaching artifacts you can pick up and run. A colleague who wants a worked example in their own field has to build it.

Nor does the published faculty-facing material cover much beyond the chat window. Almost all of it assumes a conversation with a model in a browser tab. The workflows demonstrated in this seminar — having the model write and run code, generating the slides as source, packaging a tutor as a reusable skill — are not described anywhere a faculty member would stumble onto them.

Both gaps are an opening. A school that publishes what it builds, to TRAIIL or to the Inspire framework, gets credit for work it is doing anyway.
